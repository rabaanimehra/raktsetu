Needle-free anemia screening

A bridge between a fingernail and a healthier life.

RaktSetu is a portable nail bed imaging device that screens for anemia with no needle, no lab and no wait. It is built for the frontline health workers who reach India's villages, and tested across skin tones from the start, because a screening tool that misses brown skin misses the women it was made for.

Our vision

Making anemia screening as simple as taking a photograph.

More than half of India's women are anemic, and most will never be tested. The standard check is a blood draw read by a lab, which is out of reach in many homes, villages and primary health centres. Whether you get screened should not depend on how far you live from a laboratory.

RaktSetu means "blood bridge." It connects frontline health workers and the families they visit with a fast, painless first check that shows who needs a confirmatory blood test and care.

The story

Why I built RaktSetu

When I built CardioSuraksha, my cardiac-risk screening tool for ASHA workers in rural Punjab, I made one decision that shaped everything after it: I left cholesterol out of the risk model. It is one of the strongest predictors of heart disease, but measuring it takes a blood draw, and an ASHA worker cannot draw blood at a doorstep.

Training 10 ASHA workers across 3 primary health centres taught me the same rule from the other side. She can carry a phone, a form and a referral slip. She cannot carry a lab.

Anemia breaks that rule. It is one of the most common conditions in the households she visits, affecting 58.7% of women and 71.1% of young children in Punjab, and confirming it takes a blood test. So most women are never checked until tiredness turns into something worse.

The obvious fix, a camera that reads pallor, has its own trap. Optical health devices have a documented history of working worse on darker skin: pulse oximeters missed dangerously low oxygen nearly three times as often in Black patients as in white patients (Sjoding et al., NEJM, 2020). A screening tool that fails on brown skin would fail exactly the women it is meant for.

RaktSetu is my answer to both problems: a needle-free first check designed for the doorstep, and tested across skin tones before it ever reaches one.

The gap

Why screening still falls short

Blood tests work. They are just hard to deliver at the scale and speed communities need.

Invasive by design

Finger-prick and venous tests need needles, consumables and trained staff, and they put many people, especially children, off getting tested at all.

Far from the lab

Rural and low-resource settings often lack nearby labs. Samples travel, results are delayed, and follow-up is lost along the way.

Late detection

Fatigue and pallor are easy to dismiss. Without routine screening, anemia is often found only after it has become severe.

What is RaktSetu?

A pocket-sized screening tool that reads the colour of the nail bed.

Pallor of the nail bed is a long-recognised clinical sign of anemia. RaktSetu photographs the nail bed inside a sealed chamber under fixed lighting, and a model running on the device estimates whether the person is likely anemic.

It is small, runs without internet and needs no consumables, so one health worker can screen a whole household, or a whole school, in a single visit.

How it works

Four steps. Under a minute.

No blood, no reagents, no specialist training.

Place the finger

The person rests a finger in the imaging chamber, which blocks out ambient light.

Capture the nail bed

Fixed illumination and a calibrated camera photograph the nail bed, while a MAX30102 sensor takes red and infrared readings at the fingertip.

On-device analysis

The model on the Raspberry Pi 5 analyses colour features linked to haemoglobin. Nothing leaves the device.

Clear result

The screen shows one of two outcomes: likely normal, or refer for a blood test.

Please note: RaktSetu is a screening aid, not a diagnostic device. It identifies people who should get a confirmatory blood test. It does not replace clinical evaluation.
Engineering

Every design decision, and the reason behind it.

RaktSetu is built for a doorstep in rural Punjab, not a lab bench. These four decisions follow from that.

Why the nail bed, not the skin

The nail bed carries far less melanin than surrounding skin, so blood colour shows through it on every skin tone. That makes it one of the few sites where a camera can read pallor without skin colour drowning out the signal.

Basis: Mannino et al., Nature Communications, 2018

Why a sealed chamber

Phone-camera anemia apps live or die on ambient light: a courtyard at noon and a room lit by one bulb give different colours for the same finger. A light-shielded chamber with fixed illumination removes that variable entirely.

Why on-device

Many of the villages ASHA workers cover have patchy connectivity. Running the model on a Raspberry Pi 5 means the result appears on the spot, with or without a signal, and patient images never leave the device.

Why two datasets

The model is trained on one population and tested on a completely different one: different age group, different country, a wider range of skin tones. If it holds up across that gap, it is learning anemia, not the dataset.

Version 1 · Working prototype · Complete

Designed, built and validated end to end

The enclosure was designed in CAD, the electronics built around a Raspberry Pi 5, and the screening interface designed for health workers with no technical training. Every test run is recorded in a calibration and validation sheet.

  • Compute: Raspberry Pi 5, model runs fully offline
  • Imaging: camera module in a CAD-designed, light-shielded finger chamber with fixed illumination
  • Optical sensor: MAX30102 red and infrared module
  • Interface: custom screening UI with a two-way result, likely normal or refer
  • Model: EfficientNet-B0 (transfer learning on ImageNet weights, ~4.05M parameters), fine-tuned for nail-bed pallor and exported to TensorFlow Lite (16.1 MB float32 / 4.9 MB int8) for on-device inference
  • Data: trained on Asare et al. (2022); validated on Yakimov et al. (2024)
Version 2 · In progress

Trained on Punjab, calibrated for brown skin

Version 2 moves from public datasets to data collected in the communities RaktSetu is built for, with every reading paired with a lab haemoglobin value.

  • Own dataset: consented screening across ages and skin tones in Punjab, each image paired with a confirmed haemoglobin result
  • Skin-tone calibration layer: trained on that dataset so accuracy holds from Fitzpatrick I to VI
  • Smaller and cheaper: a tighter enclosure and lower parts cost for field deployment
  • Referral built in: a positive screen generates a referral to the nearest PHC

What broke, and what I changed

The problems that shaped the current design.

Problem

The same photo, sometimes rotated or flipped by the dataset's own augmentation, turned up labelled "anemic" in one folder and "not anemic" in another. Left alone, duplicates like this can land in both train and test, making accuracy look better than it is.

What I changed

Added a perceptual-hash check across all 8 rotations/flips of every image to group near-duplicates together, then forced each group to stay inside a single split. I know it worked because the pipeline now asserts zero shared duplicate groups between train and test before training is allowed to start — 6 conflicting groups were caught and resolved this way.

Problem

Of 5,010 downloaded images, 1,625 (about a third) were silently unusable because their folder or file names didn't match an exact "anemic / non-anemic" pattern.

What I changed

Built a flexible label matcher that recognises common naming variants (Anemic, anaemic, Non-Anemic, normal, healthy, and name-embedded labels like "anemic_12.jpg"), and made the pipeline print exactly how many images were kept vs. skipped, so data loss is visible instead of silent.

Problem

The two training datasets are badly imbalanced in opposite directions — almost all Ghana images are anemic, almost all Yakimov images are non-anemic. A model can "cheat" by learning which dataset a photo is from instead of learning what anemia looks like.

What I changed

Added class-weighted loss so errors on the minority class count more, and built a mandatory per-source accuracy report rather than a single blended number. Running it proved the shortcut is still happening — strong results on Ghana, 0% sensitivity on Yakimov (see Validation below) — so this is a real, still-open problem, not one I've solved yet.

Validation

Tested where colour-based tools usually fail: across skin tones.

Most optical screening tools are calibrated on lighter skin and only checked on darker skin later, if at all. RaktSetu was tested across the skin-tone range before anything else.

Training data. Fingernail images of children under five from Ghana, with lab haemoglobin values (Asare et al., 2022, Mendeley Data).

Validation data. 250 adults spanning Fitzpatrick skin types (Yakimov et al., 2024, Scientific Data). A different age group, a different population and a wider skin-tone range than the training set.

What is not solved yet

  • The model does not yet generalise across datasets. On the Ghana test subset it performs well (86% sensitivity), but on the Yakimov subset — adults, lighter skin, a population the training data barely covers — sensitivity drops to 0%: it did not correctly flag a single anemic case in that group. This is exactly the gap the two-dataset design was built to catch, and right now the model fails it.
  • The training data is heavily imbalanced toward anemic images from one source (Ghana) and non-anemic images from the other (Yakimov), which likely lets the model shortcut on dataset-specific cues rather than learning pallor itself — the per-source gap above is consistent with that.
  • The model has been trained on public data from Ghana and Russia, not Punjab. A Punjab dataset across ages and skin tones, paired with lab haemoglobin, is the next build — and necessary before this can be trusted on adult, Indian, brown-skinned users.
  • RaktSetu flags who needs a blood test. It does not replace one.
